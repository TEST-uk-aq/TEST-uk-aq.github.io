How Black Carbon and UV help identify wood‑smoke patterns

Black Carbon measured at 880 nm responds to black-carbon-containing emissions from traffic and other combustion sources.

The 370 nm channel is more sensitive to additional UV-absorbing material associated with sources such as biomass and wood smoke.

Comparing the channels, including the UV-minus-BC difference shown here as UVPM, can reveal patterns more consistent with wood and other biomass burning. This is useful source evidence, not proof that an individual concentration came from domestic wood burning.
Negative values can occur because of the measurement and calculation methods. They do not represent negative pollution concentrations.

What is Black Carbon?

Black Carbon is a component of particulate pollution produced by incomplete combustion. It comes from several sources, including road traffic, wood burning and other fuel combustion.

Monitoring and limitations

The Black Carbon monitoring network covers relatively few locations. Readings are influenced by local pollution sources, weather and atmospheric conditions.

Monthly averages can also hide short-term pollution peaks. A monitoring station represents its immediate surroundings, not an entire town or region.

What does the evidence show?

Research at five UK monitoring sites found that wood-burning-related particulate pollution was greatest during winter evenings, particularly at weekends in urban areas. 2022 UK study

A 2026 London study identified short-term outdoor PM2.5 peaks of up to 50 µg/m³ near residential chimneys associated with domestic solid-fuel burning. 2026 London study

Defra estimates that households burning wood indoors contributed 12% of the UK's primary PM2.5 emissions in 2024. Defra emissions statistics

Wood burning and air pollution

Wood burning releases health-harming particulate pollution, including ultrafine particles (PM0.1), fine particles (PM2.5) and larger particles (PM10).

It also emits harmful gases including carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, benzene, formaldehyde and acrolein, alongside polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).

This pollution spreads beyond the chimney. Some of it also enters neighbouring homes, exposing people who do not burn wood themselves. Research on wood-smoke infiltration.

Exposure to particles and harmful gases in wood-smoke pollution is linked to heart and lung disease, stroke and lung cancer. WHO health evidence.
